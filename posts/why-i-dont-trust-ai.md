## The skill nobody teaches you in a course

CEH teaches you tools, methodology, and frameworks. It doesn't teach you how to evaluate an explanation someone — or something — gives you. In 2025, that second skill matters as much as the first, because AI tools have become a normal part of how people learn this field, and they are fluent in a way that can quietly outrun their accuracy.

This isn't a warning to avoid AI. I use it constantly to accelerate learning. It's a description of the habit I've built around using it: **treat every explanation as a hypothesis, and verify the ones that matter before I act on them or repeat them.** Here's a real example of that habit in action.

## The case: how "contained" is NAT mode, really?

While building out a home lab, I used an AI assistant to get a fast, plain-language explanation of VM networking modes — NAT, Host-only, Bridged. The explanation was good: a receptionist analogy for NAT, a clear picture of why reverse shells work, a solid breakdown of DHCP. Genuinely useful groundwork.

One claim inside it was that NAT mode keeps VM traffic "contained" — that scans run from a NAT'd VM never leave the laptop. That's a security-relevant claim. If a beginner believes it, they scan freely in NAT mode assuming the sandbox constrains them, and it doesn't — traffic goes out under the host's real IP, and the host is now the thing whose logs turn up on someone else's firewall.

I didn't accept this claim because it was well-written. I checked it, because the entire post's authority — and mine, if I repeated it — was riding on one word: "contained."

## The methodology, not just the result

Here's the actual process, which is the reusable part:

**1. Flag consequence-weighted claims first.** Not every sentence in an explanation deserves the same scrutiny. The claims that say "this is safe" or "this is impossible" are the ones where being wrong costs the most, so those get audited before anything else. A stylistic detail being wrong is a typo. A safety boundary being wrong is a liability.

**2. Notice where an analogy is doing more work than the mechanism actually does.** The receptionist metaphor was accurate for describing translation — but a receptionist also implies she screens *outgoing* calls, and NAT doesn't. The analogy's internal logic ran further than the real system does. That gap is invisible from inside the metaphor; you only find it by stepping outside the explanation and checking the mechanism directly.

**3. Reproduce it, don't re-read it.** I didn't ask a second AI to confirm the first. I booted a VM, set it to NAT, and ran:

```
ping 8.8.8.8
```

— succeeded, confirming outbound access exists. Then, instead of assuming that outbound access = leaving the laptop, I captured the traffic on the host's real interface:

```
sudo tcpdump -i <interface> icmp
```

and watched the packets leave carrying the host's real IP address. That's not an opinion about NAT. That's NAT, observed.

**4. Update the claim precisely, not just discard it.** The fix wasn't "NAT is dangerous, avoid it." It was specific: *NAT blocks inbound by default; it does nothing to outbound; the correct sandbox for active scanning is Host-only, which has no default route at all and therefore no path out to hijack.* Precision is the actual deliverable — a vague correction is barely better than the original error.

## Why this matters more in security than almost anywhere else

Wrong information about, say, a cooking technique produces a bad dinner. Wrong information about network isolation produces one of two outcomes: a beginner scans a network they don't own while believing they're sandboxed, or a professional repeats an inaccurate mental model in an interview, a report, or a client engagement.

Security work runs on boundary claims — this is isolated, this is authorized, this traffic can't leave, this system can't be reached. AI tools are trained on enormous amounts of text that states boundary claims confidently, including text that's wrong, outdated, or specific to a tool version other than the one you're using. The model has no privileged access to which confident-sounding sentence is the accurate one. Fluency and correctness are generated by the same process and don't come with different indicators attached.

That's precisely why "sounds authoritative" cannot be the test a security professional uses. The test has to be observable: can I reproduce this, in front of me, right now?

## What I actually do differently now

- Any claim of the form "X is isolated / impossible / safe by default" gets tested before I rely on it or repeat it, full stop.
- I check tool-specific and version-specific details (VMware's NAT vs VirtualBox's NAT differ meaningfully) against the vendor's own docs, not general explanation — defaults drift across versions and products in ways a general answer averages over.
- I treat an AI explanation as a fast first draft of understanding, not a citation. The citation is the command output I generated myself.
- When an analogy makes something click, I go looking specifically for the place where the analogy's implications outrun the real mechanism — that's usually exactly where the risk is hiding.

## The takeaway

Using AI well in this field isn't about trusting it more or trusting it less — it's about knowing which of its outputs need to survive contact with a terminal before you act on them. The NAT claim looked complete, felt intuitive, and was wrong in exactly the spot that mattered. Catching that wasn't luck. It was applying the same skepticism to a fluent explanation that I'd apply to an unverified claim from any other source — and then doing the five minutes of work to find out for myself.

That's the habit. Not "don't use AI." Verify what you'll act on, especially the sentence that says something is safe.
